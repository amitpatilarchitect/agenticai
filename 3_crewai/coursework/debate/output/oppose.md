I oppose the motion.

Strict laws to regulate LLMs would be a mistake because they would likely suppress innovation, entrench large incumbents, and still fail to stop bad actors. The better approach is targeted, flexible oversight of harmful uses—not heavy-handed laws on the technology itself.

First, strict regulation would freeze progress in a fast-moving field. LLMs improve constantly; laws take years to write, pass, and update. By the time a strict rule is enforced, the technology has already changed. That means regulation becomes outdated almost immediately, while slowing down useful applications in education, medicine, accessibility, and productivity.

Second, strict laws would disproportionately hurt smaller developers and open-source projects. Big tech firms can afford legal teams, audits, and compliance departments. Startups, researchers, and nonprofits often cannot. The result is less competition and more power concentrated in a few giant companies—the exact opposite of what good policy should do.

Third, strict laws create a false sense of security. Bad actors who want to spread misinformation, scam people, or generate harmful content will not obey regulations. They will use models offshore, fine-tune open-source systems, or build around restrictions. Meanwhile, law-abiding companies carry the burden, while the real threats keep operating.

Finally, we do not need strict laws on LLMs to address harm. Existing laws against fraud, defamation, discrimination, and privacy violations already apply. What we need is enforcement of those laws, plus narrow rules for high-risk uses—not broad, rigid restrictions on an entire technology class.

So the choice is not between chaos and strict control. It is between smart, targeted governance and overregulation that stifles innovation, favors monopolies, and misses the real bad actors. For that reason, I oppose the motion.
